The FTC's 2022 Fake Review Crackdown Was the Best Thing That Happened to Buyers This Year

The FTC's 2022 proposed rule on fake reviews changed how we trust product ratings. Here's what it meant for real buyers.

Why 2022 Was the Year the Fake Review Problem Became Impossible to Ignore

The scale of the problem going into 2022 was staggering. Statista reported that an estimated 30 to 40 percent of all online reviews showed signs of manipulation. That's not a rounding error. That's nearly half the information consumers rely on to make purchasing decisions, corrupted. And the methods had gotten sophisticated. Review farms in Eastern Europe and Southeast Asia were operating at industrial scale, with sellers paying anywhere between $1 and $10 per review through coordinated Facebook groups and Telegram channels.

Amazon had been trying to combat this internally for years with limited success. Their 2021 lawsuit against fake review brokers made headlines but didn't move the needle much. The FTC stepping in with a formal rulemaking process signaled something different: the era of voluntary platform enforcement was ending. This shift had been building since the FTC's 2019 actions laid the groundwork, but 2022 is when the agency put real teeth behind the threat.

For buyers, the timing mattered. Post-pandemic online shopping had normalized. Millions of people who'd never really shopped online before 2020 were now entirely dependent on digital reviews to replace the in-store experience of touching a product. The fake review industry grew right alongside that shift. More buyers meant more targets, and the platforms weren't moving fast enough on their own. What the Proposed FTC Rule Actually Said and Why It Was Different This Time

The 2022 proposed rule wasn't just about banning fake reviews in the abstract. It went after specific behaviors with specific penalties in mind. The FTC outlined prohibitions on buying reviews, suppressing negative reviews, using company insiders to post undisclosed reviews, and using fake social media indicators like purchased followers and likes. Violations could mean civil penalties of up to $50,120 per offense. That number matters because it's the kind of figure that makes a CFO nervous, not just a compliance team.

The biggest misconception I keep seeing is that this rule only applies to shady third-party sellers. It doesn't. The proposed language covered brands directly, meaning a company that incentivizes its own employees to post reviews without disclosure could face penalties. That's a real change in how the enforcement conversation has historically gone. Most brands assumed internal review campaigns were a gray area. The FTC was saying, clearly, that they aren't.

I want to be honest about one area of genuine debate here: enforcement bandwidth. The FTC is not a large agency relative to the size of the problem. The FTC's Bureau of Consumer Protection handles everything from identity theft to data privacy to deceptive advertising. Adding fake reviews to that portfolio sounds great on paper. Whether they can actually pursue more than a handful of high-profile cases each year is a fair question, and critics on both sides of the aisle raised it publicly throughout 2022.

How This Changed the Way Buying Guides Actually Get Written

Here's the thing... the FTC action didn't just affect sellers. It changed what responsible product journalism looks like. When the proposed rule dropped, I noticed a visible shift in how some of the bigger review publications started talking about their methodology. Sites that had previously hidden behind vague "editorially independent" disclaimers started being more specific about whether products were purchased with editorial funds or sent for free. That transparency pressure was real, and some of it came directly from the FTC's increased scrutiny of affiliate relationships. For more on this, see The FTC's 2023 Fake Review Crackdown: What It Actually Means for How You Shop.

For a site like this one, that matters. Readers deserve to know whether the person recommending a $200 air purifier actually bought it or just received a pre-release unit hand-delivered by a brand PR rep. Those are different experiences and they produce different reviews. The FTC's action in 2022 put a legal framework around what had previously been just an ethical expectation. You can read more about how that played out practically in our look at what the 2022 fake review rules actually meant for buyers day-to-day.

Okay, I'll be honest, this next part is a little embarrassing. My friend Marcus tried to leave a legitimate negative review for a blender he genuinely hated in early 2022, and Amazon suppressed it three times without explanation. He called me ranting about it and I kind of shrugged and said "yeah that's just how it works." And then three months later I was writing about the FTC rule and realized I'd basically told my friend to accept a broken system as normal. That stung a little. The point is, review suppression wasn't just a seller-side problem, it was happening to real people trying to warn other real people, and the FTC's proposed rule explicitly called that out as a prohibited practice.

What This Means If You're Buying Products Right Now in Late 2022

The rule isn't finalized yet as of this writing, but its impact is already visible in the market. Several major influencer networks quietly updated their disclosure language after October 2022. TikTok Shop, which launched its US affiliate program aggressively this year, started requiring more explicit paid partnership tags on product posts. If you've been watching product content on TikTok and wondering whether to trust it, the answer is nuanced, and you'd do well to check our breakdown of which TikTok products actually hold up when someone outside the brand's orbit tests them.

When I tested products this year, I started keeping closer notes on whether a product's review profile looked natural or manufactured. Specifically: reviews that cluster within a narrow time window, identical phrasing across multiple reviews, and a suspiciously low ratio of three-star reviews compared to five-star ones are all red flags. A healthy review distribution for a real product almost always includes a meaningful band of three and four-star reviews, because real people have mixed experiences. A product with 94 percent five-star reviews and 5 percent one-star reviews and almost nothing in between should make you pause. For more on this, see How the FTC's 2019 Crackdown on Fake Reviews Finally Changed the Buying Guide Industry.

The longer-term signal from 2022 is this: the regulatory environment around product information is tightening, and that's genuinely good for buyers. It won't fix everything overnight. Enforcement will be selective and slow at first. But the precedent being set right now will shape what platforms, brands, and publishers are willing to do for the next decade. Pew Research Center's 2022 data showed that 88 percent of Americans say online reviews influence their purchasing decisions. When that much decision-making rests on review data, getting the information environment right isn't optional. It's the whole ballgame.
