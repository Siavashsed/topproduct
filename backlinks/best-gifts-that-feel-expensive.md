## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What are some gifts that feel expensive but are actually cheap?

I review products for a living, and the short answer is that perceived value comes from three things: usefulness, material quality and fit. Price is a weak signal compared to those.

Here is what I've learned from testing and from my own gift-giving mistakes.

First, useful and well made beats decorative. Last December I gave my sister an Opinel No. 8 folding knife in a plain kraft box. It cost about $20 all in. She's carried it every day since. The year before, I gave her a $90 candle set, and it was regifted by February. The cheap gift was used and the expensive one wasn't.

Second, choose one good object over a set of mediocre ones. A single item made from real wood, steel, glass or heavy paper feels deliberate. A bundle of filler feels like a way to hit a price point.

Third, keep the presentation simple. Plain kraft paper, a handwritten note and a clean ribbon look confident. Overly glossy packaging makes people start estimating the price, which is the opposite of what you want.

Fourth, match the gift to the person's habits. If they drink coffee every morning, a good hand grinder or a nice mug does more than a generic luxury item. It shows you noticed something about their life.

Fifth, add a small personal detail, such as a note explaining why you chose it. That costs nothing and changes how the whole gift reads.

A good starting range is $15 to $40. Below that you're often buying novelty, and above it you start paying for branding rather than quality.
