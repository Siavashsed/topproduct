## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Are there products under $100 that perform as well as or better than much more expensive alternatives?

Yes, and in my experience as a product reviewer, it's more common than most people expect. The pattern is simple: once a product does one job well, additional money usually buys branding, finish, or features you rarely use.

Here's the example that taught me this. In March 2022 I returned a $329 pressure cooker after two weeks. It had more presets and a nicer display, but it didn't cook better. I went back to my $89 Instant Pot, which had been sitting in a cabinet since 2019, and it still sealed perfectly. Same results, a fraction of the price.

The categories where this holds up best are the unglamorous ones:

1. Cookware: a cast iron skillet will outlast almost anything and performs on par with pans costing several times more.
2. Knives: a solid chef's knife in the $40 to $80 range handles nearly everything a home cook needs.
3. Kitchen tools: a $15 instant-read thermometer is more useful than most smart kitchen gadgets.
4. Computer accessories: a comfortable mouse doesn't need to cost $150 to be accurate and reliable.

When I evaluate whether a cheaper option is truly competitive, I ask three questions. What is the one job this product has to do? Does the expensive version do that job measurably better, or just differently? And how will it hold up after two or three years of regular use?

If the premium version can't answer the second question clearly, you're likely paying for the logo.

Where price does matter is in products with complex engineering, safety implications, or heavy daily wear, such as mattresses, running shoes, and car seats. Even there, the middle tier often beats the top.
