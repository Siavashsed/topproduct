The FTC's 2022 Fake Review Crackdown: What It Actually Meant for Buyers

The FTC proposed sweeping new fake review rules in 2022. Here's what it meant for product buyers, buying guides, and trust online.

Why the FTC Moved in 2022 and Not Earlier

The honest answer is that the pressure had been building for years. The FTC's October 2022 announcement proposed civil penalties for businesses that used fake or manipulated reviews, and it followed a pattern of escalating warnings the agency had issued since at least 2019. But the 2022 proposal was different because it came with real dollar figures attached. Companies caught buying fake reviews could face fines of up to $50,120 per violation. That's not a slap on the wrist.

The backdrop matters too. By 2022, fake reviews had become a multi-hundred-million-dollar underground industry. A 2021 study published in Science Advances found that approximately 4.2% of Amazon reviews showed clear signs of manipulation, but that number ballooned dramatically in certain product categories like electronics and supplements, sometimes reaching over 30%. The FTC had the data. It had the complaints. What it finally had in 2022 was a clearer legal framework to act on both.

For context on how long this problem had been festering, our earlier piece on how the FTC's 2019 crackdown on fake reviews changed the buying guide industry covers the slower-moving earlier chapter of this story. The 2022 proposal was the next, more aggressive act.

What the Proposed Rule Actually Said (Without the Legalese)

The rule targeted several specific practices that had become standard dark-arts tactics in e-commerce. Here's what it covered:

  • Fake or purchased reviews: Paying anyone (individuals, review farms, or internal employees) to write reviews without disclosure would become explicitly illegal, not just against platform terms of service.
  • Insider reviews without disclosure: Company insiders writing positive reviews without identifying themselves as employees.
  • Review suppression: Businesses pressuring platforms to remove negative reviews or using legal threats to silence unhappy customers.
  • Fake social media indicators: Buying fake followers, likes, or engagement signals to manufacture the appearance of consumer trust.

That last one is worth pausing on. The rule wasn't just about written reviews. It covered the entire infrastructure of manufactured social proof. And if you've ever seen a TikTok account push a product with suspiciously round follower numbers and zero genuine engagement, you know exactly what I'm talking about. We've covered the overlap between social proof and product discovery before, including which TikTok-hyped products actually hold up to real testing, and the fake engagement problem is a very real filter you have to apply before trusting any of it. For more on this, see The FTC's 2023 Fake Review Crackdown: What It Actually Means for How You Shop.

The rule also placed responsibility on platforms themselves. If a company controls a review platform and is found to be suppressing negative content, the FTC made clear that liability wouldn't just sit with the brand. That was new, and frankly, overdue.

The Industry Reaction Was Predictably Messy

Some companies welcomed it. Brands that had spent years competing against cheap, review-farmed rivals saw this as a chance to level the field. A handful of mid-size consumer electronics companies I spoke to in late 2022 were cautiously optimistic. They'd been losing search rankings to products with hundreds of obviously fake five-star reviews, and they knew it.

Okay, full transparency moment here: I genuinely didn't know how to feel about the platform liability angle at first. My friend tried flagging a batch of clearly fake reviews on a major retailer's site back in early 2022, and the retailer just... didn't act. Took about six weeks of back-and-forth emails before anything moved, and even then only two reviews came down out of maybe fifteen flagged ones. So when I read the FTC's language about platform accountability, I felt something between hope and skepticism. I'm not 100% sure this works for everyone, especially smaller brands without the legal resources to pursue complaints, but the direction feels right even if the enforcement speed is another question entirely.

The skeptics, predictably, pointed out that the FTC's enforcement bandwidth is limited. The agency handles thousands of complaints across every industry. The FTC's Bureau of Consumer Protection simply doesn't have the staff to go after every review farm operating out of a server in another country. That's a fair concern. But here's the thing... the rule's real power was always going to be deterrence and platform behavior change, not prosecutions alone.

What This Means for How You Should Read Reviews Right Now

The FTC rule doesn't go into effect the moment it's proposed. There's a comment period, revision, and eventual final rule publication. So in practical terms, as of late 2022, the legal landscape hasn't fully shifted yet. But the signal is clear, and smart consumers shouldn't wait for enforcement to start adapting their habits.

When I tested products throughout 2022, I developed a rough personal filter that held up pretty well. Reviews clustered in a short time window (say, 40 reviews posted in a single week after launch) are a red flag regardless of score. Verified purchase badges help, but they're not bulletproof since sellers can reimburse purchases to game even that filter. The most reliable signal I've found is the distribution of one-star reviews. Fake review operations almost never seed negative reviews, so a product with 800 five-stars and almost zero one-stars is statistically unusual enough to warrant suspicion. For more on this, see How the FTC's 2019 Crackdown on Fake Reviews Finally Changed the Buying Guide Industry.

The 2022 proposed rule matters because it starts shifting responsibility away from consumers having to do all this detective work themselves. That's the right direction. Buyers shouldn't need to reverse-engineer review fraud just to figure out if a blender is worth $60. The FTC is, slowly, building a framework where the fraud becomes the seller's legal problem rather than the buyer's research problem. And for anyone who writes or relies on buying guides, that shift changes everything about what trustworthy sourcing looks like going forward.

Right now, before you buy anything based on its review score, take thirty seconds to check the review date distribution on the product page. If you see a sudden spike in five-star reviews around a launch date with almost no critical reviews mixed in, treat that product like it has no reviews at all and look for independent testing instead. The FTC's 2022 proposed rule is a structural fix that will take time to land. Your own skepticism is the faster one.
