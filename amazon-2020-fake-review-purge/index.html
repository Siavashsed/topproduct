Amazon Just Purged 20,000 Reviews. Here's What Broke

In 2020 Amazon deleted around 20,000 reviews after an FT probe exposed paid reviewers. What the fake review scandal means for how you shop.

If you bought anything on Amazon this year based on a wall of five-star reviews, there's a decent chance some of those stars were bought and paid for. This month Amazon quietly deleted around 20,000 reviews after the Financial Times found that seven of the platform's top ten UK reviewers were tangled up in paid-review schemes. I've been testing products and reading reviews for a living since before Prime existed, and I called it the moment I saw a $9 phone tripod pull in 4,000 reviews at a 4.9 average in under a month. That doesn't happen. Real products earn stars slowly and messily.

So here's the short version of what happened, why it matters more in 2020 than it did a year ago, and how you actually read a product page now that the star rating means less than ever. The pandemic pushed a record amount of shopping online this spring. More carts, more sellers fighting for the top slot, and a much bigger payoff for anyone willing to game the ranking. The fake review economy didn't invent itself this year. It just got a lot more crowded.

What the Financial Times actually found

The Financial Times investigation published in September traced how top-ranked reviewers were being paid, in cash or in free product, to post glowing writeups. Amazon responded by wiping the accounts and roughly 20,000 reviews attached to them. Some of these reviewers were posting dozens of reviews a day, many for cheap electronics and phone accessories shipped straight from third-party sellers.

The mechanics are grubby but simple. A seller finds a reviewer with a fat follower count and a trusted badge. The reviewer posts a five-star writeup, sometimes before the product even arrives, and gets reimbursed through PayPal or a gift card so the transaction never touches Amazon's system. Because the money moves off-platform, Amazon's own filters miss it.

When I tested this pattern myself back in 2019, I joined two Facebook groups where sellers openly recruited reviewers. Within an hour I'd been offered a $30 kitchen scale, a $22 set of resistance bands, and a Bluetooth speaker, all free, all in exchange for a five-star review posted within 48 hours. Nobody asked whether I liked the thing.

Why the star rating stopped meaning what you think

Here's the uncomfortable truth. A 4.7 average built from 3,000 reviews used to be a strong signal. Now it can be the tell. Consumer group Which? reported this year that it found thousands of suspicious five-star reviews clustered on unknown brands, often posted in bursts on the same day. A genuine product doesn't get 800 reviews on a Tuesday and 4 the rest of the month. For more on this, see Amazon Killed Honest Reviews in 2019. Here's What Actually Happened..

The pattern to watch for is velocity. Real reviews trickle in over months. Fake ones arrive in floods, usually right after a product lists, because the seller needs to hit the top of search fast before Amazon's algorithm decides where to rank it permanently.

There's a second tell that's harder to fake. Look at the three-star and two-star reviews. That's where the honest, boring, useful information lives. A person who liked a blender but wishes the lid sealed better will tell you the lid doesn't seal. Nobody pays for a three-star review, so those are almost always real.

Where the regulators are, and where they aren't

The platforms are being pushed, but slowly. In the US, this year builds on the FTC's 2019 crackdown that finally forced the buying guide industry to disclose paid relationships. That was the first time the agency went after a company for buying fake reviews outright. It set a precedent. It did not fix the problem.

In the UK, the Competition and Markets Authority opened its own investigation into misleading online reviews this year, putting Facebook and eBay on notice over the review-selling groups operating in plain sight. Facebook says it removed a batch of them. New ones pop up within days under slightly different names. It's whack-a-mole with a marketing budget.

And here's where I'll admit the picture is messier than a tidy villain story. Not every incentivized review is a scam. A legitimate reviewer who discloses a free sample and still writes an honest, critical piece is doing nothing wrong. The line isn't free product versus paid. The line is honesty versus a rating you were bribed to inflate. Regulators struggle with that distinction, and honestly, so do a lot of shoppers. How I read a product page now

I've changed how I shop this year, and I'd tell any friend to do the same. First, I sort reviews by most recent instead of most helpful, because the helpful tab surfaces the oldest padded reviews. Then I read the middle of the rating spread, not the top.

A few habits that have saved me from bad buys in 2020:

  • Run the product page through a review-analysis tool like Fakespot or ReviewMeta. Neither is perfect, but a page that drops from 4.8 to an adjusted 3.1 is telling you something.
  • Search the exact product name plus the word 'review' outside Amazon. If a hands-on writeup exists on a real publication, trust that over the star count.
  • Ignore any brand you've never heard of that has more reviews than a KitchenAid. That math doesn't add up.
  • Read the one-star reviews for shipping and safety complaints. Those are almost never faked.

This same skepticism is exactly why I'm careful with viral product hauls too. When I put together my list of the TikTok products that actually held up after I tested them, more than half the trending picks failed the moment they left the ring light. Buzz and quality are not the same thing, and 2020 has made that gap wider than ever.

Do one thing before your next purchase this week. Open the product you're eyeing, sort the reviews by most recent, and read the three-star ones first. If the complaints are specific and the praise is vague, put your card away. The star average is now the least trustworthy number on the page, and the honest signal is sitting in the reviews nobody bothered to buy.

Frequently asked questions

How many reviews did Amazon actually delete in 2020?

Amazon removed roughly 20,000 reviews tied to accounts that a Financial Times investigation linked to paid review schemes. Seven of the platform's top ten UK reviewers were caught up in it. The purge followed off-platform payments that Amazon's own filters had missed. For more on this, see Amazon's Fake Review Purge of 2021 Changed How We Buy Everything.

How can I spot a fake five-star review before I buy?

Watch the timing. Genuine reviews arrive slowly over months, while fake ones flood in on a single day right after a product lists. Read the three-star reviews for honest detail, and be suspicious of unknown brands with thousands of near-perfect ratings.

Are review-checking tools like Fakespot reliable?

They are a useful gut check, not a verdict. Tools like Fakespot and ReviewMeta estimate how many reviews look manipulated and give an adjusted score. Treat a big drop between the shown rating and the adjusted one as a warning, then confirm with a real hands-on review from outside the platform.
